As graduation approached, a friend and I prepared to apply to graduate school at POSTECH. My friend had a 4.0 GPA and went straight on to graduate school, while I had a 3.9 GPA and was rejected after taking the entrance exam. What hurt more than the 0.1-point difference was the fact that I had no next plan after building my entire future around graduate school. After graduating in 2021, I lost my motivation and spent nearly four years adrift. I relied on spending money from my mother, who had raised my sister and me on her own, and from my older sister, yet I did not know where to begin again. I could not escape labels such as an “inactive young adult” or a “boomerang child.” I was not working, but I was not at ease. As time passed, my confidence fell, and the less confidence I had, the further I drifted from taking on new challenges.

What got me moving again was a small memory from university. I still remembered how much I had enjoyed implementing physical phenomena in programs and using machine learning to classify numbers. My plan to study physics further had come to a halt, but that did not mean the interest in coding I had discovered through physics had disappeared. Rather than feeling certain that I could succeed, I was driven by the thought that I could not go on like this. I gathered what strength I had left and searched for a new path. That was how I found the Samsung Software·AI Academy For Youth.

On January 20, 2025, I began the Samsung Software·AI Academy For Youth program in Daejeon. The regional campus program was supported by the National Tomorrow Learning Card and K-Digital Training. The program was scheduled to conclude on December 11, with 1,600 hours of training spread across 215 days. On the calendar, that meant eleven months. For the first time since becoming an inactive young adult, I had something I needed to do the next day.

But I was stuck from the very beginning. Early in the program, I could not bring myself to start an assignment to build a rock-paper-scissors program. I stared at the screen for a long time without knowing where to begin. I had graduated with a physics degree and had done a little coding at university, so I assumed I would find some way to keep up. I was wrong. Having only just taken on this challenge after failing to enter graduate school, I was afraid I might fail again.

Money is an essential part of this story. My training cost was zero, and in addition to SSAFY’s training allowance, I received a separate training incentive through the National Tomorrow Learning Card. The two forms of support came from different programs, but in my life they worked together as a safety net. I mainly used the incentive for living expenses and meals, and it also helped cover the fees for certification exams such as the Engineer Information Processing and SQLD exams. Thanks to that support, I could focus on my training at the regional campus without worrying that I might run short of money for basic living expenses. For someone who had depended on family for support, it gave me the time and peace of mind to keep studying.

After getting stuck on the rock-paper-scissors assignment, I set a smaller goal: first, I would try to pass every exam in the program. I spent more than eight hours a day reading code, studied with my peers, and solved algorithm problems every day. I asked questions about what I did not know and explained again what I had come to understand. What began with Python and algorithms grew into databases, web development, AI, and team projects. Unlike the days when I did not know how to spend my time, the things I learned each day and the things I needed to study the next began to accumulate.

Once, I scored 68 on a database exam. It was a passing score, but I wondered whether this was as far as someone without a computer science background could go. Still, I could not avoid my weakest area. I studied databases again and designed and connected data myself in a project. With financial support easing the burden of application fees, I also took on the Engineer Information Processing and SQLD exams and earned both certifications, as well as Level 4 on the PCCP. In my first semester, I passed every exam, ranked second overall in Daejeon Class 2, and received an academic excellence award.

In the latter half of the program, I applied what I had learned to real problems. I extracted text from book images and used vector search to recommend similar content. In a pothole detection project, I combined YOLO and RF-DETR to improve detection performance by 8%. For a legal consultation chatbot, I went beyond implementing RAG and used RAGAS metrics to measure and improve answer quality. Through this process, I learned that AI is not simply a technology for producing plausible results. It must prepare data, validate performance, and connect those capabilities to a service that people can actually use.

Better grades did not make everything go smoothly. In one team project, I became so absorbed in my own feature that we failed to complete a working demo by the deadline. My code working and the team’s service being complete were two different things. After that, we clearly defined tasks and owners during meetings and reviewed together where the schedule was slipping. Later in the program, I led a project that classified local notices and festival information, dividing up the work and overseeing the overall schedule. I began to change from someone who built features alone into someone who completed services together with a team.

Completing 1,600 hours of training did not immediately open the door to employment. I submitted applications and was rejected. Even when I passed the document screening and reached the interview stage, I was rejected again. In one interview, I could not properly answer practical questions. The company was not asking whether I had completed a program; it wanted to know what I could actually do. After each interview, I reviewed the questions I could not answer and the skills I lacked. I had stopped after being rejected from graduate school, but this time I turned each rejection into a list of what to study next and kept applying.

That process eventually led me to join a power and energy software company. After finding a job, I had the opportunity to use both my physics background and the development skills I had built at SSAFY. I took part in developing a digital-twin-based power management platform that displays power generation, electricity usage, and the status of energy storage systems. It was my first time working with a large-scale system, but I used AI to quickly understand the flow of the existing code and data and improved the platform so that real-time operational information would be displayed accurately. As I implemented in software the relationships between phenomena and measurements that I had learned in physics, I felt the major I thought I had left behind come back to life in a new form.

I am now preparing for the Electrical Engineer certification exam after work to deepen my expertise in the electrical field. Physics, software and AI, electricity and power—things that once seemed disconnected have begun to come together in a single workplace. I did not abandon my major and become a completely different person. My goal now is to become a multidisciplinary engineer who understands the principles of equipment and energy and can use data and AI to implement them in software.

The National Tomorrow Learning Card did not guarantee me a job. I was the one who scored 68, and I was the one who could not answer the interview questions. I was also the one who studied again and kept applying. Still, the card clearly helped someone who had lost direction after a graduate school rejection and relied on family for support continue learning for eleven months without the same anxiety over living expenses. These days, I open my Electrical Engineer exam book when I get home from work. There is still much I do not know. But I no longer put my entire life on hold just because I fail or do not know something. Over those 1,600 hours, I learned where to begin learning again—and that I could keep learning all the way to the end.
