For three years, I was what the media calls an “inactive young adult.” True to the term, I avoided looking for work altogether. I had always loved physics, so I majored in it and naturally hoped to attend graduate school as graduation approached. A friend who shared that goal prepared alongside me; my friend was accepted, while I was not. What hurt was the realization that I had no plan for what came next. I depended on spending money from my mother, who had raised her children alone, and my financially independent older sister, yet I did not know where to begin again. I was resting, but I was never at ease. As time passed and my confidence fell, I drifted further away from taking on new challenges.

What got me moving again was a small memory from university. When I saw an advertisement for the Samsung Software·AI Academy For Youth, better known as SSAFY, I remembered how much I had enjoyed implementing physical phenomena in programs and using machine learning to classify numbers. The age limit happened to include people born through 1995, which meant this was my last chance to apply. My plan to continue studying had stopped, but I knew I had to do something, so I gathered what strength I had left and applied.

After reassuring myself that my mind had not gone completely rusty, I took my first test and interview in years and was fortunately accepted. On January 20, 2025, I began SSAFY training at a campus in Daejeon, where I had attended university. The regional campus program was supported by the National Tomorrow Learning Card and K-Digital Training, which allowed me to attend without worrying about living expenses. The program totaled 1,600 scheduled hours—eleven months on the calendar. For the first time since becoming an inactive young adult, I had something I needed to do the next day.

But I was stuck from the very beginning. Early in the program, during a placement test, I could not even begin an assignment to build a rock-paper-scissors program. I stared at the screen for a long time without knowing where to start. I had graduated with a physics degree and had done a little coding at university, so I assumed I would find some way to keep up. I was wrong. My fundamentals were far weaker than I had thought, and my previous experience had been built without a proper foundation. Having only just taken on this challenge, I was afraid I might fail again.

After getting stuck on the rock-paper-scissors assignment, I set a smaller goal: first, I would pass every exam in the program. I spent more than eight hours a day reading code, studied with my peers, and solved algorithm problems every day. I asked questions about what I did not know and explained what I had come to understand. What began with Python and algorithms grew into databases, web development, AI, and team projects. Unlike the days when I did not know how to spend my time, the things I learned each day and the things I needed to study the next began to accumulate.

Of course, it was not easy. Once, I scored 68 on a database exam. It was a passing score because the cutoff was 60, but I wondered whether I had become complacent. Still, I could not avoid my weakest area. I studied databases again and designed and connected data myself in a project. I also took on the Engineer Information Processing and SQLD certifications and earned both, as well as Level 4 on the PCCP algorithm assessment. By the end of my first semester, my efforts had paid off: I passed every exam as planned and finished second in my class.

The National Tomorrow Learning Card helped me greatly along the way. Because the training incentive was tied to attendance, it was not only financial support but also a commitment to arrive at the training center at a set time every day. Using it for meals and certification exam fees gave me peace of mind. In particular, one company I was interested in required an official English speaking score. During the program, I took OPIc three times and TOEIC Speaking once. The training incentive eased the burden of repeated test fees, and I eventually earned the IM3 score I had been aiming for and was able to submit my application. I could focus without worrying that exam fees would leave me short of money for basic living expenses.

In the latter half of the program, I applied what I had learned to real problems. I extracted text from book images to recommend similar content, and I combined two different vision AI models to improve road-damage detection performance by 8%. For a legal consultation chatbot, I measured and improved the quality of AI answers produced with RAG. Through these projects, I built practical AI skills and learned that AI is not simply a technology for producing results: it requires preparing data, validating performance, and connecting those capabilities to a service that people can actually use.

Completing 1,600 hours of training did not immediately open the door to employment. I submitted dozens of applications and was rejected; even when I passed the document screening and reached the interview stage, I was rejected again. In one interview, I could not properly answer practical questions. What the interviewer wanted to know was what I could actually do, and with limited experience I failed to make a convincing case. Afterward, I reviewed the questions I could not answer and the skills I lacked. I had stopped after being rejected from graduate school, but this time I turned each rejection into a list of what to study next and kept applying.

That process eventually led me to join a power and energy software company. Using the development practices and AI skills I had learned during training, I quickly traced the flow of the existing code and data and improved the system so that real-time operational information would be displayed accurately. As I implemented in software the relationships between phenomena and measurements that I had learned in physics, I felt the major I thought I had left behind come back to life in a new form. My physics background and the software and AI capabilities I gained through training had begun to meet in one workplace. I had not abandoned my major and become a completely different person. My goal now is to become a multidisciplinary engineer who understands the principles of equipment and energy and can use data and AI to implement them in software.

The National Tomorrow Learning Card did not guarantee me a job. I was the one who scored 68, and I was the one who could not answer the interview questions. I was also the one who studied again and kept applying. Still, the program and its support clearly helped someone who had lost direction after a graduate school rejection and depended on family continue learning. These days, after work, I follow the latest AI technology news and study for the Electrical Engineer certification exam. There is still much I do not know. But I no longer put my entire life on hold just because I fail or do not know something. Over those 1,600 hours, I learned where to begin learning again—and that I could keep learning all the way to the end.
